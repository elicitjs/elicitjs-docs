# Face (emotion)

<Lead>`face` encodes emotion — a datum's fields — into a face, and lets you **directly manipulate the features** to write them back: pull the mouth into a smile, widen an eye, tilt a brow. It is a **preset, not a mark**: it returns a [composite in box mode](/marks/composite#box) — a `point` head, two `ellipse` eyes, two `tickY` brows and a `curveY` mouth — each placed in the glyph's own local units. Each parameter is a channel on one of those parts, so it takes the ordinary edits and is only editable when you **bind it to a field**.</Lead>

<Section id="emotion" title="One face — drag to feel">

The emotion preset binds `mouthCurve` to `valence` and `eyeScale` to `arousal`. Grab the mouth and pull it up to smile, or drag an eye to widen it — each gesture inverts through the field's scale, and the whole face re-derives.

Because every feature is its own mark, the edits are the ones you already know: a `slide` on the mouth's curvature, a `slide` on an eye's radius. There is no `edit.face.*` namespace.

<Section id="expressive" title="Turn on more expressions">

Bind any subset of the six params in `channels`, each a plain `{ field }` with the edit you want. Unbound params stay neutral. Here every param is live: the mouth (↕ curve, ↔ smirk), the eyes (↔ aperture, ↕ squint), and the brows (↕ height, ↔ tilt).

Two params on one part means one gesture drives both — dragging an eye sideways opens it and dragging it down squints it, because `rx` and `ry` are two channels on one `ellipse`. The rule for sharing a part is that the two edits must read **different components** of the drag: one on `axis: "x"`, one on `axis: "y"`. That is also why a brow's tilt slides rather than rotating, even though it drives an `angle`. You grab a brow at its own pivot, so an angular edit would swing through most of a half-turn in a few pixels and pin the value to an extreme — the sensitivity of `rotate` comes from the pointer's distance to the pivot, and inside a glyph that distance is nearly zero.

<Section id="head" title="Move and resize the head">

The head is an ordinary `point`, so it takes an ordinary `move()`: bind `x` and `y` to fields, attach the edit, and grabbing the head drags the glyph through the plane. The eyes, brows and mouth are separate features drawn on top of it, so grabbing one of *them* still edits the expression — direct-pick dispatch routes a gesture to the touched node's feature, and nothing has to arbitrate.

<Section id="track" title="Showing a handle's track">

A positional channel in the frame is a real scale, so an edit that inverts the pointer through it — `move()` — travels exactly the range the scale spans, and no further. `guide: \{ track: true \}` draws that range: the dashed segment is the mapping itself, not a redrawing of it. Opt-in, because where the range is obvious it is only extra ink.

<Section id="creating" title="One face per day — click to add">

A face is a data glyph, so it's [creatable](/editing/existence#what-can-create) like a `point`: a plane click inverts the pointer through the x and y scales to mint a datum, and every part draws itself from it. Mark-level `edits` ride on the head part, so the create fires **once** per click rather than once per facial feature.

Here the week starts with three days filled; click an empty day to add its face — the click's height sets the `mood`, which also drives the smile. `unique({ field: "day" })` makes the day the identity, so each day holds **at most one** face.

<Section id="build" title="It's just a composite">

`face()` builds nothing you couldn't assemble yourself. Below is a smaller version written out in full — a `point` head, two `ellipse` eyes, two `tickY` brows and a `curveY` mouth, each placed in the [composite's box](/marks/composite#box).

Read `frame:` as "this number is in the glyph's own coordinates": `x` and `y` in `[-1, 1]` from the centre with y **up**, radii as a fraction of the half-size. A part that states no position sits at the centre, which is where the head goes. A field-bound channel maps the field's declared domain onto a slice of the box (`frame: [0.05, 0.19]`), and the edit inverts back through it.

Each brow is a `tickY` spanning a chord, with `worry` driving its height and its tilt. The two tilt ranges are mirrored — `[45, -45]` on the left, `[-45, 45]` on the right — so a rising `worry` tilts both brows into the same expression rather than the same direction. A `frame:` scale belongs to its own part, so the two ranges don't collide.
